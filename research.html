AI safety & research

What it takes to trust a translation system

I build production systems for a living — machine learning at Sunbird AI, payments integration at Moja Remit. At Makerere University I study whether systems like them are safe enough to put in front of people, and I am joining the Makerere Safe Intelligence Lab to do that properly. This page is about that second half.

Current work

Speech-to-speech translation for Luganda

My M.Sc. work is a direct Luganda-to-English speech-to-speech translation system, built on parameter-efficient adaptation of multimodal models. The dissertation is in for examination. The interesting part is not the pipeline — it is that almost every assumption modern speech translation rests on fails here. There is no large paired corpus. There is no established benchmark. The models that work well elsewhere were trained on data that does not include this language at all.

A translation system that is 90% right is a convenience in one setting and a hazard in another. Knowing which one you have built — and being able to prove it — is the part of this work I care most about.

So the work runs in two directions at once. One is adaptation: taking architectures like Wav2Vec and Whisper and making them work under conditions they were never designed for. The other is evaluation and safety — building the frameworks that tell you honestly how good a system is, and detecting the ways it fails.

That second direction matters more than it usually gets credit for. I am investigating bias detection in translation systems, evaluation for high-stakes contexts such as medical and legal communication, and alignment in cross-cultural settings. I work with linguists and domain experts throughout, because a system can be technically correct and still culturally wrong in ways no metric will catch.

Underneath all of it is a fairly simple commitment: democratising access to AI for East African languages, so that the people who speak them are not permanently downstream of technology built for someone else.

Starting now

Agentic AI, finance, and safety in African contexts

I am joining the Makerere Safe Intelligence Lab, whose stated mission is advancing safe, reliable and responsible AI with African contexts at the centre. My work there will focus on three things that overlap more than they look like they should.

The first is agentic AI — systems that do not merely answer but act: calling tools, moving money, taking steps on someone's behalf. The second is finance, which is where my working days already go: the integration layer of a cross-border B2B payments platform at Moja Remit, and Katale AI, a commerce agent that turns WhatsApp messages into orders and payments. The third is what AI safety means here rather than in the abstract.

An agent that takes orders by voice note, in a language with almost no training data, fails in ways a chatbot answering questions does not. The evaluation literature has very little to say about that.

I have already had to take a position on this in practice. In Katale AI the model has no authority over money at all: its tools are typed read, write and propose, pricing and validation happen in backend services, and anything consequential waits on a one-time code bound to that exact payload. Spoken approvals are refused outright, because speech recognition is the least reliable part of the chain and approval is the least forgiving place to put it.

That is one design, arrived at defensively. Whether it is the right design — how much autonomy such an agent should have, how you would measure whether the guardrails hold, what failure looks like when the user is speaking a low-resource language — is exactly what I want to study. Most safety research assumes a context, regulatory and linguistic and economic, that is not this one. I would rather examine the failure modes of systems I am actually building than inherit someone else's threat model wholesale.

Research interests

What I work on

These overlap more than the headings suggest — most of my work sits in the intersection of two or three of them.

01

AI safety & alignment

Making systems behave as intended once they leave the lab: bias detection, robustness, and alignment that holds across cultures rather than within one.

02

Agentic AI

Systems that act rather than answer — calling tools, executing transactions, taking steps for someone. What they do when an instruction is ambiguous is the interesting part.

03

AI safety in financial systems

Agents that touch money carry consequences that chatbots do not. Evaluation, failure modes and guardrails for payments, remittances and commerce.

04

Low-resource speech & NLP

Speech recognition, translation and language coverage for African languages that are poorly represented in training data, models and benchmarks.

05

Evaluation & interpretability

Honest measurement and explainable models — evaluation methodology, mechanistic interpretability, and systems whose reasoning a user can actually inspect.

06

Efficient, production-ready ML

Getting research systems into production and keeping them there: parameter-efficient adaptation, distributed training, deployment, versioning and monitoring.
